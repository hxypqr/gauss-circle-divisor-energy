   The relation between these two problems is more substantial than the similarity of their conjectured
exponents. Hyperbola decompositions express both errors in terms of fractional parts of reciprocal
functions. After Fourier expansion, one obtains double sums in an integer frequency and an integer
position variable. The Bombieri–Iwaniec method [1], adapted to the circle and divisor problems by
Iwaniec and Mozzochi [10], divides the position interval into short pieces, classifies their slopes by
rational approximations, and applies Poisson summation. A double large sieve then separates the
resulting estimate into two spacing problems.
   The first spacing problem concerns additive relations among the transformed frequencies. The
second concerns coincidences among the rational and curvature data attached to the original short
intervals. Huxley’s sequence of works [7, 8, 9] developed resonance-curve methods for the latter
problem. In the squared-radius normalization, [9] gives the exponent 131/416 = 0.314903 . . . for
both errors.
   Decoupling introduced another source of information on the first spacing side. Bourgain and
Watt [2] studied perturbed cones in connection with mean squares of the zeta function. The sharp
cone square-function theorem of Guth, Wang, and Zhang [6] was formulated through wave envelopes:
local averages of a square function at every intermediate angular scale. Guth and Maldague [5]
refined that theorem by retaining an amplitude threshold. Their refinement implies small-cap
decoupling for the cone. Li and Yang [11] combined this small-cap decoupling theorem with local
arithmetic fourth moments and Huxley’s second spacing estimates, obtaining
                      R(X), ∆(X) ≪ε X θ∗ +ε ,           θ∗ = 0.3144831759741 . . . .              (1.3)
The improvement below is within this first-spacing architecture. It does not change the resonance-
curve theorem or recover phases already removed from the short-block sum.

1.4. Organization and external inputs. Section 2 implements the discrete frequency localization
with the exact angular projections used by Guth and Maldague. Two rotated angular grids suffice,
and an entire k-ray is assigned to one grid. This avoids cutting a Fourier atom with an angular
multiplier. Section 3 proves the local density and arithmetic energy estimates, including the cross-ray
count with the all-equal-k configurations removed. Section 4 integrates the two amplitude bounds
and proves Theorem 1.2.
   Sections 5 and 6 insert the four-term estimate into the Li–Yang large-sieve interface, retain all
denominator scales, and check both admissible block lengths. Section 7 proves the Fourier and
hyperbola reductions, with the circle reduction derived through the character modulo four. The
analytic input is [5, Theorem 2]; the arithmetic input is [11, Lemma 4.4], with its stated block
construction. The classical one-dimensional estimates and the finite sawtooth approximation are
recorded explicitly where used. No higher-degree Weyl inverse theorem is an input to the proof.

                Appendix B. A direct check of the localization conventions
   There are three distinct uses of localization, and keeping them separate is important.
   First, the common cutoff χ(z/P ) replaces a periodic exponential sum by a Schwartz function. Its
Fourier support is fixed before any angular projection is taken. This support is chosen small enough
both for the cone neighborhood and for the exact integer equations in Lemma 3.3.
   Second, the two angular grids in Lemma 2.1 do not smooth the angular boundary of a Fourier
atom. Each atom is kept intact by one sharp angular projection. The assignment is made by k, so
the decomposition into same-ray squares continues to be exact after the angular split. The constants
in the external theorem are unchanged by the one rotation used for the second class.
   Third, each wave-envelope average is a genuinely weighted integral, not a periodic average. Its
pair kernel is bounded by Lemma 2.2. Only after weighted Cauchy–Schwarz and summation over
the envelope translates do we use the global fourth-moment integral. At that point the remaining
weight is exactly |χ(z/P )|4 , and its small Fourier support forces the two integer equations. The
approximate square-root equation is discarded only after those two exact equations have been
recovered.
   These distinctions also apply to complex coefficients. Local cross-ray terms may have either
sign; Lemma 4.1 handles that sign. The arithmetic counts are upper bounds for absolute values of
coefficient products after the relevant nonnegative global energy has been expanded. They are not
assertions that the original oscillatory coefficients are positive.
